Executive summary
Leaders rate themselves seven out of ten on AI knowledge - and then can't point to the return

That is the gap this paper is about, and it is not a technology gap

Across a single session of the GTA AI Quarterly, four practitioners walked through agents they have already shipped for real companies. One of them matches purchase orders for a 200-person European wholesaler for about fifteen cents a document. None of them cost more than pocket change to run. The technology works today

What separates the operators getting value from the ones still waiting is not the model, the vendor, or the budget. It is three organizational behaviors that most companies never put in place

Here is what the field evidence says:

IN ONE LINE
The technology works today. The organization is the constraint
01

The constraint is organizational, not technical - the agents in this paper run for cents per task and were built in weeks, not quarters. The bottleneck sits upstream of the technology, in how companies share knowledge, measure time, and decide what to build

02

Efficiency use cases are live and measurable right now - a document-matching task that took a senior person 90 to 240 minutes now completes in under a minute at roughly fifteen cents, plus about ten minutes of human review. That is not a projection, it is in production

03

The 2024 "AI SDR" flop reset the market to co-pilot - tools sold as finished autonomous products shipped as MVPs and underperformed. The market corrected back to humans signing off on AI-drafted work, and that is where the durable value is

04

Three behaviors separate the top 10% from the bottom 90% - structured internal knowledge sharing, horizontal 1% daily productivity gains tied to real time tracking, and executive-level discipline on build versus buy

05

ROI is easiest when the problem is well-defined and the founder or CFO is in the room - efficiency gains on an existing process are straightforward to price. Greenfield revenue plays are harder, and enterprise complexity is hardest of all

The rest of this paper is the evidence behind those five points, in the words of the people who built the systems

The expectation gap

There is a strange dichotomy at the top of most companies right now

Leaders are excited, and leaders are anxious, often the same leaders at the same time. McKinsey's latest marketing survey puts hard numbers on it - almost every CMO is excited, most are anxious, and four in five see a risk to their own job. Meanwhile fewer than one in ten organizations capture value across a full workflow

Excitement is high. Deployment is low. That is the gap

96%
report high excitement with AI
71%
express anxiety about it
80%
perceive a risk to their own role
<10%
capture value across end-to-end workflows
TABLE 1 · CMO SENTIMENT VS CAPTURED VALUE · MCKINSEY GLOBAL MARKETER SURVEY, MARCH 2026, N=521

Part of it is that people overestimate what they know. The panel kept circling the same dynamic - the one where you either think you understand AI better than you do, or you don't know what you don't know. "Some people don't understand," Zoltan said. "Some people don't want to understand and live in a bit of denial."

You see the expectation gap most clearly in the pricing conversation. Tsvetelin has been in the room when a client reads a report, sees a headline number, and tries to renegotiate

"You have quoted X on the project. I have read a report which said AI is able to automate 75 to 80 percent. Can you do this project for 15 to 20 percent of the quoted price?"

A CLIENT, TO TSVETELIN

That is the expectation gap in one sentence. The headline says 80 percent, so the buyer assumes the price should drop 80 percent, and the actual work of scoping, cleaning data, and keeping a human in the loop disappears from view

Practitioners don't have this problem. When you talk to the people building the systems, they know exactly what AI can and cannot do. The confusion lives with the non-technical decision-makers who read the reports and set the expectations

Closing that gap is not a training problem you solve with a slide deck. It is a demonstration problem, and the rest of this paper is the demonstration

USE CASE 1
Automate the friction, protect the relationship

Marc's work is relationship-driven B2B influencer marketing, and his point is the counterweight to all the automation enthusiasm: transcription is not context

Marc uses AI to enhance existing workflows rather than replace them. The note-taking, the transcription, the meeting recap that used to eat the hour after every call - that is friction, and it automates cleanly

What does not automate is having been on the call. Having seen the faces. Having read the room. Some jobs are done between people, and technology makes the friction lower without ever replacing the human in the middle

That distinction - automate the friction, protect the relationship - is the practical version of "keep a human in the loop". In influencer marketing the entire product is trust between people, so the line between the two is unusually easy to see. Most businesses have the same line, they just have not drawn it yet

The rest of the panel's cases push automation as far as it will usefully go. This one is about knowing where it stops

USE CASE 2
The news ranking agent

A PR firm needed to surface the most relevant news for each client, out of the hundreds of articles published daily. The easy version is a keyword alert. The useful version is a ranking problem

Hundreds of articles a day, dozens of clients, and a team whose job is to know which three pieces matter to whom. A keyword alert answers the wrong question - it tells you the client was mentioned, not whether anyone should care

Tsvetelin's team built a weighted algorithm across 10 to 12 parameters - the domain authority of the publisher, where the keyword sits in the piece, and, crucially, the contextual relevance of the article to what the firm is actually doing for that client. Then they layered AI models on top to process and rank

The context of the client engagement became the lens that decided what mattered. The same article can be noise for one client and the day's most important signal for another - no keyword can tell the difference, and the engagement context can

The output is not a feed. It is a short, ranked list a human can act on before the morning coffee goes cold

USE CASE 3
The immigration sponsor agent

A clean example of a brand-new opportunity: in the UK, the government publishes a register of licensed visa sponsors, refreshed every single day - somewhere between 50,000 and 100,000 records

No human is calling through that. The data was public and free, and it was completely unworkable by hand - which is exactly why nobody worked it

Tsvetelin's team built an agent that processes the daily register, identifies newly licensed sponsors that fit the law firm's ideal client, and every morning hands the business development team a shortlist

Not just names - a draft outreach script for each one, and, when connected to enrichment tools, recent context about the company to personalize the approach

The firm was not doing this before. There was no manual process to speed up, no headcount to save. The entire motion exists because processing a hundred thousand records a day became free

USE CASE 4
From CRM entropy to customer intelligence

One lesson shared with every case in this paper: the agent is only as good as the data underneath it

Running a media company at the scale of hundreds of thousands of readers across several domains and apps created a very specific problem - understanding who those readers actually were

The first practical use case was unifying that data: enriching it, segmenting by psychosociographic and behavioral cohorts, understanding lifetime value and cost of acquisition per segment, and matching on-site behavior to the demographic cohort it belongs to. Then using AI to lift conversions on top of that unified base

For most SMEs under about five million in revenue, this is the use case they fall in love with first - understanding what clients do on your website, who they are, and how to enroll them into a funnel that accelerates the go-to-market motion

None of it works on a messy foundation. CRM entropy is the number one bottleneck in the field - if the underlying data is a mess, you cannot extract value from it, and you certainly cannot match it against an enrichment tool

USE CASE 5
The fifteen-cent purchase order

Zoltan's first move was not to build anything. It was to sit down with the CEO and find the real bottleneck

The company is a wholesaler - around 200 employees, five or six offices across Europe, somewhere between 700 and 800 purchase orders coming in every month. A team of people processed all of it by hand

The hard part was matching product IDs: the external IDs on the incoming orders never matched the company's internal ones, so every line had to be reconciled manually. For a senior person, a single sixteen-page order took 90 to 120 minutes. For a less experienced person, two to four hours

The agent does the same job in under a minute, at a model cost of about fifteen cents. This is not a fully autonomous system - you still want a human reviewing the output, which adds roughly ten minutes

Call it fifteen minutes end to end against up to four hours. That is the shape of a real efficiency win

"You just need the right people."

Zoltan's takeaway: get the right people in the room before you buy anything - people who are AI literate, who understand how the technology works and what governance needs to be around it. Call it an AI center of excellence, a committee, or a fractional hire; the label does not matter. What matters is that someone can tell a solvable problem from a fantasy. And when you have something to show, demo it on the client's own documents - explanation changes no minds, the demo changes all of them

WHAT TO DO NOW
Put someone AI-literate in the room before you buy
Demo on your own documents, not slides
Keep a human review step in every loop
Price from real unit costs, not headline percentages
